# Proposed evaluation and decision-performance coding

First pilot task feasibility, response variation, timing, and scoring in one upper-level course. Use independent pre/post forms and a delayed form two to four weeks later. Report paired changes and uncertainty without claiming causation. Select and pilot additional unfamiliar records from the existing snapshot before research; T1/T2 are candidates, not established equivalent forms.

A subsequent comparison gives both conditions the same records, briefs, vocabulary, time, and discussion opportunities. Both conditions teach project applicability and when sufficient evidence resolves one concern. The comparison instructor models a narrative alert summary; students write summaries and discuss them in pairs. The proposed activity organizes the same content through four memo prompts, required peer critique, and justified revision. See [matched lesson directions](comparison_lessons.md). Both groups receive unscaffolded assessments asking for an action and evidence-based justification. This estimates the combined sequence, not individual components.

Balance identity/extraction limitations, unresolved applicability, and sufficient verified exercise evidence allowing resolution of a specific concern. Exclude A-D and D+. Prespecify acceptable alternatives and task demands; counterbalance forms. Public tasks may be familiar to students, so formal research requires unseen forms and an exposure check.

Primary decision-performance score: one point each for identifying the actual limitation or evidence sufficiency, connecting it to the project decision, and recommending a targeted action or justified resolution of a specific concern. Report by task type. Analytic rubric totals, unsupported applicability claims, unnecessary verification, and memo revision are secondary. Two assessors should score independently with condition/timing masked where feasible, report agreement before adjudication, and calibrate beforehand.

An improved rubric score without better task-specific actions would not support the central claim. Increased caution everywhere would not demonstrate calibrated judgment. A future study must establish reliability and validity; task equivalence, prior knowledge, clustering, and instructor effects remain threats. Obtain applicable institutional review and consent and separate research participation from course grading.

## Feasible sequence and analysis decisions

1. Prepare unseen assessment forms and task-specific acceptable rationales from the existing snapshot. Include identity/extraction, unresolved applicability, grouping/naming, and sufficient verified exercise evidence. Pilot task comprehension, timing, variation, and assessor agreement; avoid treating forms as equivalent before checking them.
2. Run a one-course feasibility pilot with an independent pre-task before instruction, the proposed 65-minute activity, an independent post-task, and a delayed task two to four weeks later. Pre/post assessment time is additional research time; it is not all included in the classroom activity's 65 minutes. Avoid pre-task feedback before the post-task.
3. If recruitment and the number of teaching groups permit, compare the complete sequence with the matched alert-summary lesson, allocating by tutorial/session to reduce pair-work contamination. Keep time, material exposure, vocabulary, and discussion opportunity comparable. If too few groups are available, remain at the feasibility stage.

Each unfamiliar task scores 0-3 on the three prespecified decision-performance criteria. Prespecify an equal-weight participant mean across task types as primary, regardless of the number of records per type. Report type-specific results and secondary analytic rubric totals. Use pilot variation, feasible enrollment, and clustering to plan sample size before efficacy testing. Record exposure to public tasks, prior experience, allocation departures, delayed-task attrition, and missing responses. Report uncertainty; do not claim causal effects from a one-group pilot or a confounded intact-class comparison.

## Proposed contrast task
A candidate pair uses the same unfamiliar snapshot report with two clearly constructed project briefs: one with unresolved component identity, and one stipulating verified identity plus a selected version outside a confirmed affected range. Counterbalance order without feedback between responses. Successful adaptation targets identity corroboration in the first and resolution of a specific concern in the second. Generic information requests on both fail the targeted-action criterion. Reusing a report isolates responsiveness to project evidence within the pair, but does not itself demonstrate transfer to other reports; include additional unseen records and monitor carryover/order effects. The pair is proposed, not a validated instrument.

## Design hypothesis

We hypothesize that explicitly explaining and defending why different evidence requires different actions improves decisions on unfamiliar records. The proposed comparison tests the combined memo, critique, and justified revision sequence against a matched narrative-summary lesson. Both groups receive the case contrasts, so this comparison cannot establish the independent benefit of contrasting cases. The hypothesis concerns decisions on unfamiliar records. Better memo wording alone, or more information requests on every task, would not support it. Estimate differences from the matched comparison condition and their uncertainty before drawing conclusions about an instructional advantage. The study tests the complete sequence; it cannot attribute an effect to case order, prompts, or peer critique separately.

## Assessment task requirements

These are requirements for future unseen research forms, not claims that the public practice tasks already constitute such an instrument.

- Include missing information that does not change the decision being assessed. For example, a missing severity score need not block resolution of a range-based concern when the supplied identity and range checks already exclude the selected version.
- Include reassuring details that do not establish project applicability. For example, complete package fields or a collection-workflow review label do not resolve an unknown project version. Preserve original snapshot values; place any additional project facts in clearly labeled constructed briefs.
- State evidence and project facts without announcing the desired action or labeling a brief as sufficient or unresolved. Use the same neutral action-and-justification instructions across task types. Do not make one task type identifiable merely by reassuring language, response length, or the presence of blanks.
- Balance the reasoning demands across forms and pilot whether students can distinguish consequential facts from distractions. Vary the specific surface details so success does not rest on remembering a practice example. These task-design choices require pilot checks, not an assumption that the forms are equivalent.

The sufficient-evidence condition must still supply enough facts to support a limited conclusion. Making the expected action less obvious does not justify hiding evidence students need or demanding speculation about unspecified risks.

## Task-specific decision scoring guide

Before scoring, prepare a guide for each task identifying the claim under review, the supplied evidence, the unknowns that block the recommendation, what each useful check resolves, and acceptable alternative actions. Distinguish concerns within the task from unrelated risks that remain outside its scope. Assessors must not demand an exhaustive security assessment for resolving one specific concern.

Award the action point only when the response addresses the blocking concern or correctly explains why none remains for the limited claim. A conditional recommendation can earn credit if its conditions require the necessary checks before acceptance. A pause can earn credit if the response specifies what must be resolved. An unconditional acceptance that leaves a blocking applicability question unanswered earns no action point, however fluent its explanation.

Examples for calibration, not learner results:

| Task state | Recommendation | Action-point rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Package identity remains unresolved. | Check only the selected version against a range whose package identity is still uncertain, then accept. | 0: the proposed check does not resolve the identity needed to apply the range. |
| Package identity remains unresolved. | Confirm the report-to-component mapping, then check the candidate version and relevant conditions before acceptance. | 1: the conditional plan addresses the blocking concern. |
| Identity and range checks exclude the selected version; severity score is missing. | Resolve this range-based concern and continue ordinary adoption review. | 1: the missing score does not change the supplied version comparison. |
| Identity is credible and package fields are complete, but project version is unknown. | Accept because the report is detailed. | 0: completeness does not establish whether the selected version is affected. |

Record the factual reason for each judgment and allow alternative plans that resolve the same concern. The other two primary points still assess recognition of the relevant limitation or sufficiency and its connection to the project. These examples clarify the proposed scoring process; they do not demonstrate measurement validity or actual engineering outcomes.
